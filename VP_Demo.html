What we built in the pilot

Why it's shaped like this

  • The shared pieces — auth, audit, encryption, database, UI, testing — live in one set of packages that every app inherits. Reviewed once, used everywhere.
  • A new app starts by copying a template that already passes CI. The first app is expensive; the second and tenth are minimal development with configurations.
  • It's one repo for source, but each app deploys on its own, rolls back on its own, and has its own database. A bad deploy in one app can't take down the others.
The point: Devin didn't produce three one-off scripts, it produced a lane. The template is what makes the next ten tools cheap.

Power Apps grows per seat; the Devin lane barely moves with user count. In the middle band the people-costs roughly cancel out.

How we read this

  • At a thousand users per tool, seat math settles it for the Devin lane.
  • If we can't review code, Power Apps wins by default — that's why A3 exists.
  • In between, cost isn't the decider. Org shape and the kind of tool are.
  • Both vendors changed pricing within six months of each other. Neither pricing model is safe to assume long-term.
Undercut the "we'll save $250k" expectation gently: at our size the prize is capability and the falling cost of tools #2 through #13.

What we recommend: route every request

And the flag panel stays on Power Apps

  • Building the replica taught us what a flag system really is: SDKs in about sixty services, a 99.95% availability promise, and on-call. That's a runtime product, not an admin panel.
  • Knowing that price is exactly why we're leaving it where it is. We built the replica to find out whether moving was worth it; it isn't, today.
  • It's a reversible decision: we revisit only if the replica clearly outperforms and the license audit shows Premium spend growing. Both, not either.
The gate routes per request, without needing the VP in the room. Flags is the honesty showcase — we built it to learn why not to move it.
